my perspective on ai

September 2026

I'm writing this after a swarm of agents resolved the Navier-Stokes problem. This has been a really interesting moment in mathematics and for a lot of people has put into further question their motivation for being in the field. If you're a mathematician then you know this, but there is a lot of creativity that goes into structuring and finding the correct pathways when trying to solve a problem. There's a concurrent fear that AI does not correctly communicate this creativity and that by using these AI systems to solve difficult problems, we are losing out on intuition gained in the proof that could lead to contributions in other fields.

I think that one of the key strengths of LLMS, at least at the moment, is that it has a very large breadth of knowledge. For the new proofs that aren't counterexamples, they've been found by finding overlooked connections between disparate subfields. It's very difficult to know "all" of math (and I would argue impossible) because there's so much knowledge that has been gained in different subfields which require different styles of thinking and different prerequisites. It seems like the only current lead that humans have over AI is in matters of depth. But, I have no doubt that as AI gets stronger than this human advantage will disappear for this too. Furthermore, I believe that in the future, if not now, that AI will be able to correctly synthesize and communicate the intuition in novel proofs that is required to bridge fields and develop new areas of math. That's what its good at: finding connections between different areas.

This is existentially very scary for someone who likes doing math research. I like solving difficult problems and I enjoy finding different approaches to understanding information. One of the most fun parts about research is being able to find ideas, prove they're true, and then using those ideas to find newer ideas. I feel like its similar to creating the scaffolding for a very pretty building. However, at least for me, it's very difficult to distinguish what truly makes my style of building "better" than an AI assisted or even fully automated building. If the results are the same, does it really matter how beautiful the building is?

For some mathematicians, there's an inherent beauty to proofs, whether that be the simplicity of the result or the clarity and foresight of the mathematical arguments. Personally, I don't really have this idea of beauty. I think that as long as a proof is logically correct then its just as valid as a simpler proof. In my opinion, its better to know that something is true and then work it down to generalize and make it more simple. That's how a lot of problem solving works. AI is also able to take complicated proofs and after rounds of iteration make them more simple. It's difficult to see where the role of the mathematician is.

Here's my case: I think that being a math researcher will involve more management and creation of research systems with varying levels of human guidance. I'm still not sure what level humans will have in this but my hope is that there's something special about us that will keep us around in math. This isn't being presented as an optimistic outcome and I'm sure most mathematicians are not looking to work in this manner, but I think this is where the direction of the field is going. I'm just an undergraduate math major, but this is how I see the wind blowing. If this ends up being true, there will need to be a reckoning in academia for how the entire system functions. Just like how math researchers had to adapt to the invention of the internet, math researchers will have to adapt to the advent of the AI age.

At the end of the day, as someone who wants to pursue a PhD, I want to keep doing research. Even if the style of research changes, my motivation isn't solely from the problem solving aspect. I also enjoy expanding the field of known human knowledge and being able to add new, previously unknown concepts to our shared collective storage of ideas. I think that this will still be possible with LLMs.

To go back to the Navier-Stokes problem, AI didn't end the field of PDEs. With this result, it's given more room than ever. Now it seems that the Navier-Stokes equations do not perfectly model the movement of fluids. This gives so much more leverage to come up with different models and also further investigate and come up with simpler results.

I really don't want to fear an AI future. I think it's best to look at things with hope and try to adapt. That's all we can do since change is the default state of life and to give up on change is to stop living.
